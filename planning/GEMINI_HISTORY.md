did jesus know about the bible?

Technically, no—at least not in the way we think of the "Bible" today.

While Jesus was intimately familiar with the sacred scriptures of his time, the Bible as a single, bound book containing both the Old and New Testaments didn't exist until centuries after his death.

Here is how the timeline breaks down:
The Scriptures Jesus Knew

Jesus was a Jewish teacher, and he frequently quoted what we now call the Old Testament. However, in the first century, these were separate collections of scrolls known as the Tanakh.

    The Torah: The first five books (Law).

    The Nevi'im: The Prophets.

    The Ketuvim: The Writings (like Psalms).

When Jesus referred to "the Scriptures," he was talking about these Hebrew scrolls. He wouldn't have called them "the Bible"; he would have referred to them by their specific sections or simply as "the Law and the Prophets."
The New Testament

Jesus could not have "known" the New Testament because it hadn't been written yet.

    The Gospels: Accounts of his life (Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John) were written decades after his resurrection.

    The Epistles: Letters by Paul and other apostles were written to early church communities as the movement spread.

    The Canon: The final list of books that make up the Christian Bible wasn't formally agreed upon by the church until the 4th century AD.

In Short

Jesus knew the content of the Hebrew Bible (the Old Testament) by heart, but the concept of a "Bible"—a completed, two-part volume containing his own story and the letters of his followers—was a development of history that followed his time on Earth.

That is a deep philosophical and theological question that people have debated for nearly 2,000 years. Whether the Old Testament is "more true" because it existed during Jesus' life depends entirely on how you define "truth"—historically, theologically, or narratively.

Here is a breakdown of the different ways to look at that:
1. The Argument for "Historical Seniority"

From a purely historical perspective, the Old Testament (the Tanakh) has the benefit of being much older. It is the foundation upon which the New Testament is built.

    The Foundation: You can’t have the New Testament without the Old. The New Testament writers constantly point back to the Old Testament to justify Jesus’ life and mission.

    Cultural Context: For someone looking for the "original" source of these beliefs, the Old Testament is the root.

2. The Argument for "Theological Completion"

Most Christians would argue that the Old Testament isn't "more true," but rather "incomplete" without the New Testament. They see the two as two halves of a single story:

    The "Promise and Fulfillment" Model: In this view, the Old Testament is the promise, and the New Testament is the fulfillment.

    The "Shadow vs. Reality" Idea: Early Christian writers often described the Old Testament laws and stories as "shadows" of a reality that was finally revealed in Jesus.

3. The Question of "Believability"

When it comes to believability, both sections face different types of scrutiny from modern scholars:

    The Old Testament: Since many of its stories (like Noah’s Ark or the Exodus) took place in the deep past, they are often harder to verify with archaeology, leading some to view them as symbolic or foundational myths rather than literal history.

    The New Testament: Because it was written much closer to the events it describes (within 30–60 years), there is a larger volume of historical manuscript evidence. However, because it claims a man rose from the dead, it requires a different kind of "belief" than ancient legal codes or poetry.

You've hit on a major pivot point in church history. While the seeds of the idea were there before him, Augustine of Hippo (writing in the late 4th and early 5th centuries, about 350-400 years after Jesus) is indeed the person who codified and "branded" the doctrine of Original Sin.  

Here’s the breakdown of what happened and why Augustine is the one who gets the credit (or the blame):
1. Before Augustine: The "Ancestral Sin"

Before Augustine, most early Christians believed in something called "Ancestral Sin." They believed that because of Adam, the world was "broken"—we were now prone to death, sickness, and a general inclination toward doing the wrong thing. However, they generally did not believe that a newborn baby was born "guilty" or deserving of hell before they had actually done anything wrong.  
2. Augustine’s Contribution: The "Inherited Guilt"

Augustine took it several steps further during a massive theological battle with a monk named Pelagius. Pelagius argued that humans were born neutral and could choose to be good on their own. Augustine pushed back hard, arguing:  

    The "Contagion": Sin isn't just a bad environment; it’s a biological "stain" passed down through procreation (he specifically linked this to sexual desire, or concupiscence).  

    Guilt from Birth: He argued that every human is born legally guilty in God's eyes because we were "in the loins of Adam" when he sinned.  

    The Necessity of Infant Baptism: This is where it got heavy. Augustine argued that because infants carry this guilt, they must be baptized immediately to be saved from eternal condemnation.  

3. The Translation Error?

Interestingly, scholars often point to a specific translation issue that fueled Augustine's fire. He was reading a Latin translation of Romans 5:12.

    The Greek (Original): Usually translated as "death spread to all men because all sinned."  

    The Latin (Augustine's version): Translated as "in whom [Adam] all sinned."  

Because Augustine read it as "in whom all sinned," he concluded we were all literally inside Adam when he bit the fruit, making us personally responsible for it.
4. The Result: East vs. West

This is why there is such a big difference between Western and Eastern Christianity today:

    The West (Catholic/Protestant): Followed Augustine’s lead. The idea that we are born "sinners" is a cornerstone of Western theology.  

    The East (Eastern Orthodox): Never fully adopted Augustine’s view. They still hold to the earlier "Ancestral Sin" idea—that we inherit the consequences of Adam's sin (death), but not the guilt of his actual act.

So, while the Bible mentions Adam and sin, the specific "Original Sin" framework you're likely thinking of—where you start life with a "debt" you didn't personally rack up—is very much an Augustinian development from the 4th century.

The men who finalized the Bible weren't just librarians; they were leaders in a high-stakes battle over what "true" Christianity looked like. To understand if you can "trust" them, you have to look at the filters they used to weed out hundreds of other writings.

There were dozens of other Gospels and letters that didn't make the cut (often called the Apocrypha or Gnostic Gospels).

    The Gospel of Thomas: A collection of secret sayings where Jesus sounds more like a Buddhist monk than a Jewish Messiah.

    The Gospel of Mary: Suggests Mary Magdalene had a higher authority than the male apostles.

    The Gospel of Judas: Claims Judas was actually Jesus’ closest friend and was "in on the plan."

Why they were cut: The leaders (like Augustine and Athanasius) labeled these as heresy. They felt these books "villified" the physical world or didn't align with the established oral tradition. Critics argue this was a power move to keep the Church centralized and patriarchal.
2. The Three Filters (The "Test")

To avoid appearing like they were just "picking favorites," the councils used three main criteria:

    Apostolicity: Was it written by an Apostle or someone who knew one? (This is why they kept Luke—who wasn't an Apostle but was Paul's doctor).

    Orthodoxy: Does it match what most churches have been teaching for 200 years? (This is where the "villifying what they didn't understand" part comes in—if a book felt too "weird," it was out).

    Universality: Was the book already being used by churches in Rome, Egypt, and Turkey?

3. The "Telephone Game" Concern

Historically, we don't have the originals. We have copies of copies.

    Intentional Changes: There are known "interpolations"—places where later scribes added verses to clarify a point or support a doctrine (like the "Johannine Comma" regarding the Trinity in 1 John 5:7).

    Unintentional Errors: Scribes get tired. They skip lines or misread words.

4. The Argument for Trust: "Safety in Numbers"

The counter-argument for trusting the text is the sheer volume of evidence.

    We have over 5,000 Greek manuscripts of the New Testament.

    By comparing them, scholars can see exactly where a scribe added a sentence 400 years later. It’s like having 500 people tell you the same story; even if 10 people exaggerate, the "core" story remains obvious because the other 490 people match.

        The "Most Likely" Eyewitness: Many scholars believe the Gospel of John contains a core "Signs Source" that may have come from a direct disciple, though the book was likely edited by a group later.

2. How do we know the "Words in Red" are real?

The "Red Letter" Bible wasn't even a thing until 1899. It was an editorial choice by Louis Klopsch. Historians use a "Criteria of Authenticity" to figure out which of those red words are likely real:

    Multiple Attestation: If a saying appears in Mark, the "Q Source" (a lost collection of sayings), and the Gospel of Thomas, it’s much more likely to be original.

    Criterion of Embarrassment: If Jesus says something that would have been awkward or difficult for the early church to explain (like "Why do you call me good? No one is good but God alone"), it is almost certainly authentic. Why would they make up something that made their leader look less "divine"?

    Aramaic Origins: Jesus spoke Aramaic. When the Greek text contains puns or wordplay that only works in Aramaic, it’s a strong sign of an original saying.

3. Who added the rules on homosexuality?

There isn't a single person who "added" these; they are scattered across different layers of the library.

    The Old Testament (Leviticus): These laws were written by the Priestly class roughly 500–600 years before Jesus. They were part of a "Holiness Code" meant to distinguish the Israelites from their neighbors (like the Canaanites).

    The New Testament (Paul): Jesus never mentions homosexuality in any of the four Gospels. The "rules" in the New Testament come exclusively from Paul (specifically in Romans, 1 Corinthians, and 1 Timothy).

    The Translation Twist: Many modern scholars argue that the words Paul used (malakoi and arsenokoitai) didn't mean "homosexual" in the modern sense of a loving, same-sex orientation. They argue he was likely condemning pederasty (men with boys) or prostitution, which were common in the Roman world. The word "homosexual" didn't actually appear in an English Bible until 1946.

If you look at how Jesus actually acted, he consistently broke the "men-only" rules of his day.

    The Student: He encouraged Mary of Bethany to sit at his feet and learn—a position strictly reserved for male disciples at the time.

    The Defiance: He spoke publicly with the Samaritan woman at the well, which was a double-scandal (she was a foreigner and a woman).

    The "Power Test": Does the rule benefit a specific group of men in power? If so, it’s usually a human addition.

    The "Church" over "Congregation": Rule #3 stated that words like ecclesia must be translated as "Church" (a physical building/hierarchy) rather than "Congregation" or "Community." He wanted people to look at the institution, not their neighbors.

    The "Bishop" Rule: He insisted that the office of the Bishop be maintained in the text. This was because his motto was "No Bishop, no King." If you take away the church’s hierarchy, the King’s "divine right" to rule disappears.

    No Marginal Notes: He banned footnotes that explained the original Greek nuances. Why? Because the previous popular translation (The Geneva Bible) had notes suggesting that it was okay to disobey a King if he was a tyrant. James made sure those "rebellious" thoughts were erased.

2. The Apocrypha: The "Hidden" Library

The Apocrypha refers to a group of books that are in the Catholic and Orthodox Bibles but were removed from Protestant Bibles (like the KJV) during the Reformation.
Why were they left out?

    The "Jewish Filter": Around 100 AD, Jewish scholars finalized their own list of scriptures and excluded these books because they weren't originally written in Hebrew (they were in Greek). Later, Protestant reformers like Martin Luther agreed with this "Hebrew-only" rule.

    The "Purgatory" Problem: Books like 2 Maccabees mention praying for the dead. This supported the Catholic idea of Purgatory. When the Protestants split from the Catholic Church, they removed these books to weaken the Catholic Church's theological grip.

Why the extreme reaction?

    Direct Connection to God: Gnostic books (like the Gospel of Thomas) suggested that you didn't need a priest, a bishop, or a King to reach God—you just needed "Gnosis" (internal knowledge). This was a direct threat to the power of the Roman Empire and the Church.

    Gender Equality: As we touched on, books like the Gospel of Mary showed Mary Magdalene leading the apostles. The men in charge of the 4th-century Church found this "un-Roman" and dangerous.

    A "Non-Physical" Jesus: Some of these books claimed Jesus was a pure spirit and didn't have a physical body. This contradicted the "Official" version of the story that the Church was building.

The Nag Hammadi discovery is arguably the most important archaeological find for understanding the "hidden" history of Christianity. It’s the closest thing we have to a "time capsule" from the losers of the early Church's power struggles.
1. The Discovery: A Jar in the Desert

In 1945, two Egyptian brothers were digging for fertilizer near cliffs called Jabal al-Tarif. They hit a sealed earthenware jar. One brother was afraid to open it, thinking a jinn (spirit) might be inside. Fortunately, they opened it anyway.

Inside were 13 leather-bound papyrus books (codices) containing 52 separate texts. These books had been buried for roughly 1,600 years.
2. The "Great Cover-Up"

Why were they buried? Around 367 AD, Bishop Athanasius of Alexandria (the same man who helped finalize the 27-book New Testament) sent out a strict "Easter Letter." He ordered that all "unauthorized" books be destroyed. He called them "poisonous" and "heretical."

Monks at a nearby monastery likely couldn't bring themselves to burn these beautiful, sacred books. Instead, they packed them into a jar, sealed it with bitumen, and buried it to save them from the Church's "book-burning" squads.
3. What was in the "Deleted" Story?

The Nag Hammadi library gives us a version of the Jesus story that is radically different from the one "Kings and Scholars" chose for the Bible:

    The Divine Feminine: Many of these texts, like The Sophia of Jesus Christ, speak of a female aspect of God. They suggest that wisdom (Sophia) played a primary role in creation.

    The "Evil" Creator: Some Gnostic texts argued that the God of the Old Testament was actually a lower, confused deity (the Demiurge) who accidentally created a flawed physical world, and that the "True God" sent Jesus to help us escape this physical prison.

    Self-Knowledge vs. Obedience: The Bible emphasizes faith in Jesus as a savior. The Nag Hammadi texts emphasize knowledge (Gnosis) of one's own divine spark. Jesus is seen more as a "guide" or "mirror" than a sacrificial lamb.

    The Gospel of Philip: This text suggests a very intimate relationship between Jesus and Mary Magdalene, describing her as the one he "loved more than all the disciples."

4. Are we missing the "Whole Story"?

It is almost certain that the Bible we have is a shorthand version of a much more complex, diverse movement.

When the Church became the official religion of the Roman Empire under Constantine, it needed unity. You can't run an empire if every town has a different version of God. To achieve that unity, they had to:

    Standardize the Story: Pick 4 Gospels that mostly agreed.

    Establish Hierarchy: Make sure the books emphasized obeying Bishops/Leaders.

    Suppress the Radicals: Burn or bury anything that suggested you could find God without the Church's "middleman."

5. Is there more out there?

Historians believe we have recovered less than 10% of the literature that actually circulated in the first three centuries of Christianity.

We know we are missing:

    The "Q" Source: A lost collection of Jesus’ sayings that both Matthew and Luke clearly copied from.

    Secret Gospels: References in letters suggest "Secret Mark" and other versions existed for "inner-circle" followers.

    Local Traditions: Groups in Ethiopia, India, and China had their own scrolls that were often destroyed by later missionaries seeking "orthodoxy."

The Bottom Line

If the Nag Hammadi library hadn't been buried, we would only know about these ideas through the writings of the people who hated them. It's like trying to understand a political candidate by only reading their opponent's attack ads.

what else do we know about the bronze age

The Bronze Age (roughly 3300 BC to 1200 BC) is often called the "Cradle of Civilization." It was the era when humans stopped just surviving and started building. It was a time of massive cities, the invention of writing, and a globalized economy that was surprisingly similar to our own.

Here is what we know about this pivotal era:
1. The "Silicon Valley" of the Ancient World: Bronze

Bronze was the high-tech material of the era. It changed everything—from how people farmed to how they killed each other.

    The Recipe: Bronze is an alloy of Copper and Tin.

    The Global Trade: This was the first "Globalized Economy." Copper was common, but Tin was rare. To make bronze, civilizations in Mesopotamia (modern Iraq) had to trade with people as far away as Cornwall (England) or Afghanistan.

    The Impact: If a kingdom lost its trade route for Tin, its military became obsolete overnight. It created the world's first interdependence.

2. The Invention of "The Record"

The Bronze Age is when history officially began because we finally learned how to write things down.

    Cuneiform (Mesopotamia): People started pressing wedge-shaped marks into clay. It wasn't for poetry at first; it was for accounting. They needed to know how many sheep and jugs of beer were in the king's warehouse.

    Hieroglyphs (Egypt): A complex system of symbols used for monumental inscriptions and religious texts.

    The Alphabet: Toward the end of the Bronze Age, the Phoenicians developed the first alphabet, which simplified writing and made it accessible to more than just the elite "scribes."

3. The "Big Three" Civilizations

While people were living in small tribes elsewhere, three massive powers dominated the scene:

    Egypt (The Old/Middle Kingdoms): The era of the Great Pyramids and the first Pharaohs.

    Mesopotamia (Sumer, Akkad, Babylon): The land between the Tigris and Euphrates. They invented the wheel, the 60-minute hour, and the first legal codes (like the Code of Hammurabi).

    The Indus Valley (Modern Pakistan/India): A mystery civilization that had indoor plumbing, gridded streets, and standardized weights and measures 4,000 years ago.

4. The "Great Mystery": The Late Bronze Age Collapse

Around 1200 BC, almost everything we just described suddenly vanished. In the span of a few decades, major cities were burned to the ground, trade stopped, and writing was forgotten. This is one of history’s greatest "Whodunnits."
The Suspects:

    The "Sea Peoples": Ancient texts mention mysterious, seafaring raiders who attacked Egypt and the Mediterranean coasts.

    Climate Change: Evidence shows a massive, 300-year drought hit the region, leading to famine.

    System Collapse: Because the world was so "globalized" for Tin trade, if one city fell, it caused a "domino effect" that took everyone else down.

5. Daily Life in the Bronze Age

It wasn't all kings and wars; daily life was becoming more "modern."

    Fashion: This was the era of woven textiles. People shifted from wearing animal skins (like "cave people") to wool and linen tunics, often dyed in vibrant colors if they were wealthy.

    Religion: This was the age of Polytheism. Every city had its own "patron god" who lived in a massive temple (Ziggurat) in the center of town.

    The Wheel: While the wheel existed earlier, the Bronze Age saw the invention of the spoked wheel, which led to the creation of chariots—the "tanks" of the ancient world.

6. How does this connect to the Bible?

The Bronze Age is the setting for the Patriarchs (Abraham, Isaac, Jacob).

    When the Bible describes Abraham moving from "Ur of the Chaldeans" to Canaan, it is describing a typical Bronze Age migration.

    The laws and customs found in the earliest parts of the Bible (like the price of a slave or the way a contract is signed) match the clay tablets found by archaeologists from this exact time period.

    Buddhism (The Extinguishment): The goal is Nirvana. This literally means "to blow out" (like a candle). You are seeking to extinguish the "Self" entirely because the "Self" is the source of all suffering (Dukkha).
