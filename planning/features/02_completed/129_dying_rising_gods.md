## Problem Statement

The Parallel Stories view introduces the dying-rising god pattern as one comparison among several. This topic deserves its own page. The dying-rising god archetype — a deity who dies, descends to the underworld, and returns — is one of the most widely distributed mythological patterns in the ancient world, and it is the pattern most frequently cited in popular culture arguments about the origins of the resurrection narrative.

This article needs to do two things well: (1) present the genuine parallels accurately and with their primary sources, and (2) present the genuine differences honestly, so readers get a real comparison rather than either a dismissive "nothing to see here" or an overstated "Jesus is just Osiris."

## Content Outline

### Section 1 — The Pattern Defined

What "dying-rising god" actually means as a scholarly category. The term was developed by James George Frazer (*The Golden Bough*, 1890) and has been both enormously influential and significantly criticized. The academic debate: Jonathan Z. Smith argued in "Dying and Rising Gods" (Encyclopaedia of Religion, 1987) that many gods categorized as "dying-rising" actually don't rise — they are simply absent (Persephone in Hades) or their resurrection is contested in the sources. Mark the genuine cases; acknowledge the contested ones.

Scope note: This article covers the five figures most cited in academic and popular discussion (Osiris, Dionysus, Adonis, Tammuz/Dumuzi, Baal) plus two commonly misrepresented cases (Mithras, Attis). Persephone, Inanna herself, and Heracles are excluded — their death-and-return stories are structurally different or secondary to the pattern under discussion.

### Section 2 — The Major Figures

**Osiris (Egypt, c. 2400 BC+)**
- Murdered and dismembered by Set. Isis reassembles his body and temporarily resurrects him to conceive Horus. Osiris then rules the underworld as judge of the dead — he does not return to the living world.
- Key difference from Christian resurrection: Osiris's resurrection is partial, temporary, and into the underworld as its ruler — not into ongoing earthly or cosmic life. The Osiris myth is about cyclical renewal (the Nile flood, agricultural death and rebirth) not personal resurrection.
- Genuine parallel: a god defeated, killed, and restored to life through divine action, with cosmic consequences.

**Dionysus (Greece, various traditions)**
- Multiple death traditions: torn apart by Titans (Orphic tradition); a mortal incarnation who dies and is reborn. His worship involves ritual consumption of wine (his blood/essence) and ecstatic union with the divine.
- The virgin birth claim: in the Semele tradition, Dionysus is born of a mortal woman and Zeus — sometimes cited as a virgin birth parallel. Semele was not a virgin; she was a mortal who had relations with Zeus.
- Genuine parallel: a god associated with death, resurrection, divine parentage, and a ritual meal with his followers.

**Adonis (Phoenician/Greek)**
- Dies from a boar's wound; spends part of the year in the underworld with Persephone, part above ground with Aphrodite. An agricultural cycle myth — his descent and return mark the seasons.
- Not a resurrection in the personal/cosmic sense: Adonis doesn't "rise triumphant." He alternates between absence and presence. Jonathan Z. Smith's point applies here.

**Tammuz / Dumuzi (Mesopotamia, c. 2000 BC+)**
- The shepherd god beloved of Inanna/Ishtar. Dies and descends to the underworld. Ishtar descends to rescue him (the Descent of Inanna). In some versions, Tammuz is released for half the year — the source of Ezekiel 8:14's reference to "women weeping for Tammuz."
- Genuine parallel: a dying god mourned by a devoted female figure, with annual festivals of mourning and celebration.

**Baal (Canaanite)**
- Killed by Mot (death), descends to the underworld, and is restored to life. His wife Anat fights Mot to restore him. The annual agricultural cycle.
- Relevant to the Hebrew Bible: the Baal cult was the primary religious competitor to YHWH-worship in the period of the Judges and Kings. The prophets' condemnation of Baal worship is partly a condemnation of dying-rising god religion as incompatible with YHWH's nature.

**Mithras (Roman mystery cult, 1st–4th c. AD)**
- The most frequently cited figure in popular "Jesus is pagan" arguments — and the clearest example of Smith's critique applied. The primary sources (Plutarch, cave iconography, Statius) contain *no death-resurrection narrative*. The central Mithraic image is the *tauroctony* (bull-slaying), not a death and return of Mithras himself. The resurrection parallel is absent from the ancient sources; it appears in early-20th-century comparative religion writing and has been passed forward without verification.
- Key difference: Mithraism was a 1st-century AD Roman mystery cult — it postdates the earliest Christian texts. The direction of influence, if any, runs the wrong way for the borrowing argument.
- Why include it: readers who arrive at this page specifically because they heard "Jesus is just Mithras" deserve a direct, sourced response.

**Attis (Phrygian/Greek)**
- A vegetation deity associated with Cybele. Castrates himself and dies; in some late traditions (Firmicus Maternus, 4th c. AD) he is resurrected. The resurrection element appears primarily in Christian-era polemical sources, making it difficult to date independently.
- Contested case: the late attestation of the resurrection element makes Attis a weak parallel for pre-Christian influence. Often grouped with Adonis as a seasonal-absence myth rather than a true dying-rising narrative.

### Section 3 — The Genuine Differences

This section matters as much as the parallels. The comparison argument often collapses genuine differences:

1. **Chronology:** Most dying-rising gods predate Christianity. This is true. But chronological priority does not establish causation. The question is whether the 1st-century Jewish movement that produced Christianity was consciously borrowing from mystery religion traditions — a claim that requires evidence of contact and influence, not just temporal precedence.

2. **The nature of the resurrection:** Osiris rules the underworld. Dionysus returns to cyclical life. Jesus, in the Christian claim, rises bodily and permanently into a transformed existence that is not the underworld and not just "back to normal life." The specificity of the Gospel accounts (empty tomb, physical wounds, eating, the disciples' recognition) is different in character from the mythological traditions.

3. **The Jewish context:** The resurrection expectation in 1st-century Judaism (Pharisaic tradition, Daniel 12, the Maccabean martyrs' hope) is the immediate context for the disciples' interpretation of Jesus's death and reported resurrection — not Greek mystery religion, which was viewed with suspicion by observant Jews.

4. **The historical claim:** The Gospel accounts present the resurrection as a specific historical event with named witnesses, a specific location, and a specific time — not as a cosmic/seasonal myth. The nature of the claim is different from the mythological narratives.

### Section 4 — What the Pattern Reveals

The recurring pattern across cultures suggests something about human narrative need — what Joseph Campbell called the monomyth. The death-and-return structure appears because it maps onto universal experiences: the seasons, sleep and waking, the near-death experience, grief and recovery.

But the pattern's universality changes the question, rather than answering it. The fact that this narrative form existed across cultures explains why it was *available and meaningful* to early Christians and their audiences — not whether any specific instance of it is historically true. A form can be universal and a particular instantiation can also be a unique historical event. The two claims operate at different levels.

The productive question the comparison opens is not "Jesus is just X" or "there's nothing to see here" — it's: *Why did this particular form carry so much weight in the ancient imagination, and what does that tell us about what human beings have always believed they needed from their gods?* That question is worth sitting with regardless of where a reader lands on the historical claims.
