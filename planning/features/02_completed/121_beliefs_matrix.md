---

## Problem Statement

Readers arrive at questions like "how does Islam view the afterlife differently from Christianity?" or "do Hindus believe in heaven?" with no single place to compare traditions side by side. The existing site covers these questions only incidentally through the Bible-focused content. This page creates a structured cross-tradition comparison grid — descriptive, not prescriptive — that maps how eight major traditions answer the same five fundamental questions.

**Editorial stance:** This page maps belief systems as they understand themselves. We describe what each tradition teaches, not which is correct. Where traditions have internal diversity (Protestant denominations, Hindu schools, Buddhist branches), we use the mainstream or most widely-held position with a note that variation exists.

## The Matrix

### Rows (Traditions)
1. Protestant Christianity (baseline: mainstream evangelical/Reformed)
2. Catholic Christianity
3. Eastern Orthodox Christianity
4. Islam (Sunni baseline, noting Shia variation where significant)
5. Judaism (traditional/Orthodox baseline, noting Reform variation)
6. Hinduism (Vedantic/mainstream, noting school diversity)
7. Buddhism (noting Theravada/Mahayana distinction where it matters)
8. Taoism (classical/philosophical Taoism)

### Columns (Belief Topics)
1. **God / The Divine** — What is the nature of the ultimate reality?
2. **Afterlife** — What happens when you die?
3. **Salvation / Liberation** — How does a person reach the good outcome?
4. **Sacred Texts** — What is authoritative, and how literally?
5. **Jesus** — Who is he in this tradition?

### Cell Content Format
Each cell: 2–3 sentences summarizing the tradition's position + a "Deep dive →" link to the relevant article. Cells that have no meaningful answer (e.g., Taoism on "Jesus") get an honest note: "Jesus is not a significant figure in classical Taoism."

## Matrix Content (Cell Summaries)

### Column 1: God / The Divine

| Tradition | Summary |
|---|---|
| Protestant | One God in three persons (Father, Son, Holy Spirit) — the Trinity. Personal, intervening in history, knowable through scripture and prayer. Omnipotent, omniscient, omnipresent. |
| Catholic | Same Trinity as Protestant. Additionally emphasizes God's presence through the Church, the sacraments, and Mary's intercessory role. |
| Eastern Orthodox | Same Trinity, with a key difference: the Spirit proceeds from the Father *alone* (not "and the Son" as the Western churches hold). This *filioque* dispute triggered the 1054 Great Schism. |
| Islam | Strict oneness (*tawhid*) — no Trinity, no partners, no physical form. The 99 names of Allah describe his attributes. The Quran explicitly rejects the idea that God begets or was begotten. |
| Judaism | YHWH — strict monotheism. The Shema ("Hear O Israel, the Lord our God, the Lord is One") is the foundational declaration. The divine name is so sacred it is not spoken aloud; many write "G-d" rather than the full word. |
| Hinduism | Brahman — the ultimate impersonal reality underlying all existence. Multiple personal deities (Vishnu, Shiva, Devi) are understood as manifestations of the one Brahman. Often described as *henotheism* (many gods, one supreme). |
| Buddhism | Non-theistic — the Buddha did not claim divine authority and set aside questions about God as unhelpful to the path of liberation. Some Mahayana traditions venerate Buddha-figures in ways resembling worship. |
| Taoism | The Tao — the underlying principle of existence. Impersonal, unnameable, self-arising. "The Tao that can be spoken is not the eternal Tao." No creator god; the universe generates itself from the Tao. |

### Column 2: Afterlife

| Tradition | Summary |
|---|---|
| Protestant | Heaven (eternal life with God) or Hell (eternal separation from God) after death, assigned at the Last Judgment. Bodily resurrection at the end of time. No intermediate state in most Protestant traditions. |
| Catholic | Heaven, Hell, or Purgatory — a process of purification after death for those who die in God's grace but not fully purified. Prayers for the dead can assist those in Purgatory. |
| Eastern Orthodox | Heaven and Hell, with prayers for the departed. No formally defined doctrine of Purgatory. Salvation is understood as ongoing transformation (*theosis*) that continues beyond death. |
| Islam | Barzakh (an intermediate state between death and judgment), then the Day of Judgment (*Yawm al-Qiyamah*), followed by Jannah (paradise) or Jahannam (hell). The bridge *As-Sirat* must be crossed; deeds are weighed. |
| Judaism | *Olam Ha-Ba* (the world to come) and bodily resurrection are affirmed in traditional Judaism. *Gehenna* functions as a temporary purification (up to 12 months for most souls), not an eternal hell. Afterlife is far less central to Jewish theology than to Christian or Islamic theology. |
| Hinduism | The soul (*atman*) is reborn into a new body (*samsara*) based on accumulated *karma*. The ultimate goal is *moksha* — liberation from the cycle entirely. The good outcome is not heaven but escape from rebirth. |
| Buddhism | Rebirth across multiple realms (human, deva, animal, hungry ghost, hell) based on karma — but not reincarnation of a permanent soul (*anatta*, no-self). The goal is *nirvana*: the cessation of craving and the end of the rebirth cycle. |
| Taoism | The individual returns to the Tao — the distinction between self and cosmos dissolves. Ancestor veneration is practiced. Some schools pursue physical or spiritual immortality. Classical Taoism is more focused on living rightly than on afterlife reward. |

### Column 3: Salvation / Liberation

| Tradition | Summary |
|---|---|
| Protestant | Grace alone (*sola gratia*) received through faith alone (*sola fide*). Salvation is God's gift, not a human achievement. Works flow from faith as evidence, not as its cause. |
| Catholic | Grace through faith + the sacraments + works of mercy. Baptism removes original sin; confession restores grace after mortal sin; the Eucharist sustains it. Purgatory completes what is unfinished at death. |
| Eastern Orthodox | *Theosis* — becoming progressively like God through a lifetime of sacraments, prayer, fasting, and love. Not a legal transaction but a transformation of nature. |
| Islam | Submission to God's will expressed through the Five Pillars + sincere faith + God's mercy. No one can guarantee paradise — salvation is ultimately God's decision. Works and faith both matter. |
| Judaism | Covenant faithfulness: Torah observance, *teshuvah* (repentance), *tzedakah* (righteous giving), and *tefillah* (prayer). Salvation in the Christian sense is not the primary category — the focus is living rightly in this world under the covenant. |
| Hinduism | *Moksha* — liberation from samsara — through one of several paths: *karma yoga* (right action without attachment), *bhakti yoga* (devotion to a personal deity), *jnana yoga* (knowledge/wisdom). Different schools emphasize different paths. |
| Buddhism | *Nirvana* through the Noble Eightfold Path: right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration. There is no self to save — the goal is to dissolve the illusion of permanent selfhood that drives craving. |
| Taoism | *Wu wei* — effortless action in alignment with the Tao. Not striving, not forcing. The person who lives in harmony with the Tao is already where they need to be. Liberation is not an achievement; it is the removal of what blocks natural flow. |

### Column 4: Sacred Texts

| Tradition | Summary |
|---|---|
| Protestant | The Bible (66 books) — *sola scriptura* (scripture alone is the ultimate authority). Interpreted by the individual believer, aided by the Holy Spirit. No binding tradition or teaching office. |
| Catholic | The Bible (73 books) + Sacred Tradition + the Magisterium (the Church's teaching authority, exercised by bishops in union with the Pope). Scripture and Tradition are co-equal sources. |
| Eastern Orthodox | The Bible (76–78 books) + Holy Tradition + the decrees of the Seven Ecumenical Councils. The Church's living tradition interprets scripture; scripture does not stand alone. |
| Islam | The Quran (the direct word of God, transmitted through the Prophet Muhammad, preserved without variant) + the Hadith (verified sayings and actions of Muhammad) + the Sunnah (Muhammad's practice). The Quran exists in one text; there are no significant variant manuscripts. |
| Judaism | The Torah (the Five Books of Moses, written) + the Talmud (the oral Torah, recorded in the Mishnah and Gemara) + Midrash + centuries of rabbinic commentary. The oral Torah is considered equally authoritative with the written. |
| Hinduism | The *Shruti* ("heard" — the Vedas and Upanishads, considered eternal) and the *Smriti* ("remembered" — the Bhagavad Gita, Mahabharata, Ramayana, Puranas). No single authoritative text; different schools emphasize different scriptures. |
| Buddhism | The *Tripitaka* (Pali Canon) for Theravada; a much larger body of Mahayana sutras for Mahayana traditions; Vajrayana texts for Tibetan Buddhism. No single authoritative Buddhist scripture — canon varies significantly by tradition. |
| Taoism | The *Tao Te Ching* (Laozi, 6th–4th century BC) + the *Zhuangzi*. Both are understood as pointing at the Tao, not containing it — the text is a finger pointing at the moon, not the moon itself. |

### Column 5: Jesus

| Tradition | Summary |
|---|---|
| Protestant | Son of God, second person of the Trinity, fully human and fully divine. His death atones for sin; his resurrection defeats death. Will return at the Second Coming. |
| Catholic | Same core as Protestant. Additionally: his body and blood are truly present in the Eucharist (*transubstantiation*); Mary is his mother and the Church is his body on earth. |
| Eastern Orthodox | Same core Christology. Emphasis on the Incarnation as the moment God entered matter — the physical world is thereby sanctified. |
| Islam | The Prophet Isa ibn Maryam — born of a virgin, a great miracle-worker, one of the five greatest prophets. Not divine, not the Son of God. The Quran denies the crucifixion: another was made to appear like him. He was raised to God alive and will return before the Day of Judgment. |
| Judaism | A Jewish teacher and healer in the 1st century. Not the Messiah — the messianic criteria (Temple rebuilt, world peace, ingathering of the exiles) were not fulfilled. Opinions range from sincere reformer to false prophet. Not a significant figure in Jewish theology. |
| Hinduism | Not a figure in mainstream Hinduism. Some Hindu teachers have described him as an *avatar* (divine descent) or a great *yogi*. These are individual interpretations, not Hindu doctrine. |
| Buddhism | Not a figure in Buddhist teaching. Some writers have noted structural parallels between Jesus and the *bodhisattva* ideal (enlightened being who remains to help others). These are comparative observations, not Buddhist doctrine. |
| Taoism | Not a figure in Taoist teaching or cosmology. |

## Page Architecture

### Sections (in order)

1. **Introduction** — The editorial stance: descriptive, not prescriptive. These are the traditions' own claims about themselves. A brief note on internal diversity (we use mainstream positions; variation exists within every tradition). 2–3 paragraphs.

2. **The Matrix** — Full 8×5 grid. On desktop: full table with sticky row/column headers. On mobile: scrollable with tradition filter tabs (select one tradition to see all its beliefs in a column view, or select one topic to see all traditions' answers).

3. **How to Use This Page** — Short editorial note: the matrix is an orientation. Each topic has a deep-dive article for readers who want the full picture. Click any cell to go deeper.

4. **Notes on Tradition Diversity** — A brief sidebar-style section acknowledging that each "tradition" contains multitudes: Catholic liberation theology differs from Vatican conservatism; Theravada and Mahayana Buddhism disagree significantly; Reform Judaism differs from Orthodox. The matrix uses the mainstream or historically dominant position in each case.

5. **Cross-links** — → Afterlife deep-dive (`beliefs-afterlife`) / → Nature of God (`beliefs-god`) / → Salvation & Liberation (`beliefs-salvation`) / → Jesus Across Traditions (`beliefs-jesus`) / → Parallel Stories (`parallels`) for Greek, Norse, Egyptian, and Mesopotamian tradition overlap

## Design Notes

- **Desktop:** CSS grid table. Sticky first column (tradition name) and sticky header row (topic name). Each cell is concise; hover reveals a "Read more →" prompt.
- **Mobile:** Tab-based filter. Two filter modes: by tradition (show one tradition across all topics) or by topic (show one topic across all traditions). Simple JS tab switching — no new framework.
- **Styling:** Use existing `.bv-table` / `.scroll-table` as baseline. Extend with sticky header/column behavior via CSS `position: sticky`.
- **Cell links:** Each cell's "Deep dive →" link uses `data-nav` to route within the SPA.
- No new JS dependencies beyond the tab filter (can be 15 lines of vanilla JS).
