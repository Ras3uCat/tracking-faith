## Problem Statement

"How does a person reach the good outcome?" is the practical heart of every religion. But the answers are not just different versions of the same answer — they reflect entirely different frameworks for what is wrong with the human situation, what the obstacle is, and what overcoming it looks like.

For Christianity, the problem is sin and the penalty it incurs; the solution is atonement. For Buddhism, the problem is the illusion of a permanent self and the craving it generates; the solution is insight. For Judaism, the problem is unfaithfulness to the covenant; the solution is repentance and return. For Taoism, the problem is not a problem at all — it is unnecessary straining against what is already natural. Each of these frameworks is internally coherent and produces radically different practical ethics, prayer forms, and community structures.

This page maps those frameworks accurately, side by side.

## The Traditions

### Protestant Christianity

**The core claim:** Salvation is by grace alone (*sola gratia*) received through faith alone (*sola fide*). It is entirely God's gift — not a human achievement, not a reward, and not something that can be partially earned and then completed by God. This was the central insight of the Reformation.

**Key doctrines:**
- **The problem:** Original sin — humanity's fundamental corruption and guilt inherited from Adam (Romans 5:12). Every person is born in a state of enmity with God, unable to choose God by their own unaided will (total depravity in Reformed theology).
- **The solution:** The atoning death of Jesus Christ. Christ took the penalty for human sin (substitutionary atonement) and credited his righteousness to believers (imputation). Salvation is forensic — a legal declaration of righteousness (*justification*) — not a transformation of substance.
- **Faith:** The instrument of receiving salvation, not its ground. Saving faith is trust in Christ, not mere intellectual assent. In Reformed theology, faith itself is a gift of God — even the act of believing is enabled by grace.
- **Works:** "For by grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God, not a result of works, so that no one may boast" (Ephesians 2:8–9). Works flow *from* salvation as its evidence and fruit — they do not contribute to it.
- **Assurance:** Reformed theology allows for assurance of salvation — believers can know they are saved. Arminian and Wesleyan traditions allow for the possibility of falling away.
- **Election:** In Reformed/Calvinist theology, God elects specific individuals for salvation before creation (*predestination*). Arminian theology holds that election is conditional on foreseen faith — God foreknows who will believe.

**The great Reformation debate:** The Lutheran/Reformed position was forged in direct opposition to what Luther saw as Catholic works-salvation. The phrase *sola gratia, sola fide* appears nowhere in the Bible — it is a theological synthesis that claims to express what the Bible teaches as a whole. Its opponents (Catholic and then Arminian) argued it was an overcorrection.

### Catholic Christianity

**The core claim:** Salvation is through faith, sacraments, and works of charity — understood not as competing systems but as the unified expression of a life of grace. God's grace is the source and ground of everything; human cooperation with that grace is real and necessary.

**Key doctrines:**
- **The problem:** Original sin *and* actual sin. Catholics agree with Protestants that the human condition is fallen. They disagree that justification is purely a legal declaration — it also involves genuine interior transformation.
- **Justification as transformation:** Catholicism, following the Council of Trent (1547), teaches that justification is not merely imputed but genuinely imparted — God doesn't just *declare* a person righteous, He makes them righteous through grace. This is a fundamental difference in the doctrine of justification.
- **The sacraments:** Seven channels through which God's grace is ordinarily communicated: Baptism (removes original sin, initiates into the Church), Confirmation (strengthens faith), Eucharist (nourishes ongoing spiritual life), Confession/Reconciliation (restores grace lost through mortal sin), Anointing of the Sick, Holy Orders, and Marriage. The sacraments are not merely symbolic — they effect what they signify.
- **Mortal vs. Venial sin:** Mortal sin (grave matter, full knowledge, full consent) severs the person's relationship with God and requires sacramental confession to restore. Venial sin weakens the relationship but does not sever it.
- **Purgatory:** The final purification of those who die in God's grace but are not yet fully purified. Not a second chance; not for those in hell. Purgatory completes the transformation that wasn't finished in this life.
- **James 2:24:** "You see that a person is justified by works and not by faith alone." This text is a primary battleground between Catholic and Protestant interpretations. Catholics cite it directly; Protestants argue James means "the kind of faith that is genuine justifying faith" produces works — the faith and works cannot be separated.
- **Merit:** In Catholic theology, a person in grace can "merit" additional grace through acts of charity — not because they earn God's love, but because God freely chooses to reward the acts done in His grace. This is the concept Luther attacked most vigorously.

### Eastern Orthodox Christianity

**The core claim:** *Theosis* — deification, becoming by grace what God is by nature (2 Peter 1:4: "partakers of the divine nature"). Salvation is not a transaction but a transformation. The goal is not legal acquittal but genuine union with God.

**Key doctrines:**
- **The problem:** Orthodox theology understands the fundamental human problem not primarily as *guilt* (legal) but as *mortality and corruption* — human nature became subject to death and its effects (passions, desires, confusion) through the Fall. The deepest wound is not legal penalty but the damage done to human nature.
- **The Incarnation as salvation:** In Orthodox thinking, salvation began at the Incarnation — God uniting Himself with human nature in the person of Christ. "God became man so that man might become God" (Athanasius). The cross and resurrection are the completion of this, not the whole mechanism.
- **Synergy:** Salvation requires cooperation (*synergeia*) between God's grace and human will. God does not override human freedom. "God without us cannot save us; we without God cannot be saved ourselves" (Augustine, cited approvingly in Orthodox contexts despite his Western provenance). This is distinct from both Protestant monergism (God acts alone) and Pelagian self-salvation (human effort alone).
- **The sacraments as participation:** Orthodox worship is immersive — the Divine Liturgy is understood as participation in the heavenly liturgy, not merely a memorial. The Eucharist is the body and blood of Christ; receiving it is genuine participation in divine life.
- **Ongoing transformation:** Theosis is not a one-time event. It is the work of a lifetime — and, for those on the path, continues beyond death.
- **Hell:** Orthodox theology resists describing hell as a place God sends people. The more common Orthodox theological picture is of God's love being experienced as unbearable by those who have refused to be transformed by it. Hell is self-chosen exclusion, not imposed punishment.

### Islam

**The core claim:** Salvation through submission (*islam*) — alignment of one's whole life (external practice and internal intention) with the will of God, expressed through the Five Pillars and sincere faith, completed by God's mercy on the Day of Judgment. No one can guarantee paradise; salvation is ultimately God's decision.

**Key doctrines:**
- **The problem:** Islam does not have a doctrine of original sin in the Christian sense. Adam and Eve sinned, repented, and were forgiven — their sin is not transmitted to their descendants. Each person begins life with a clean state (*fitra*, natural disposition toward God) and is accountable only for their own deeds.
- **The Five Pillars:** The structural backbone of Muslim practice: Shahada (declaration of faith: "There is no god but Allah and Muhammad is His messenger"), Salat (five daily prayers), Zakat (obligatory almsgiving — 2.5% of wealth above the nisab threshold), Sawm (fasting during Ramadan), and Hajj (pilgrimage to Mecca, once in a lifetime if able).
- **Faith and works:** Both are required. Faith without works is insufficient; works without faith are dead. The Quran repeatedly pairs belief (*iman*) with righteous deeds (*amal salih*) as the formula for success.
- **God's mercy (*rahma*):** God's mercy is vast — the Prophet Muhammad said "God has one hundred mercies, and He has sent down one of them among jinn and humans and animals and insects, by means of which they are compassionate to one another and by means of which wild animals show kindness to their young. And He has kept back ninety-nine mercies to deal with His slaves on the Day of Resurrection" (Muslim). The hope of God's mercy is a constant theme.
- **No intercession without God's permission:** The Prophet Muhammad can intercede for believers on the Day of Judgment — but only by God's permission. There is no independent mediating power. This maintains strict tawhid.
- **Tawbah (repentance):** Sincere repentance directly to God at any moment can wipe out sins. No priest, no sacrament, no mediator required. "Say: O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of the mercy of Allah" (Quran 39:53).

### Judaism

**The core claim:** *Teshuvah* (return, repentance) within the covenant. The Jewish framework does not ask "how do I get to heaven?" — it asks "how do I live faithfully in this life under God's covenant?" Salvation in the Christian sense is not the primary category.

**Key doctrines:**
- **The covenant:** God chose Israel at Sinai and gave the Torah — not as a means of earning salvation, but as the way of life for a people already in covenantal relationship. Torah observance is the response to election, not its cause.
- **The 613 commandments (*mitzvot*):** Traditional Judaism counts 613 commandments in the Torah (248 positive, 365 negative). These range from Sabbath observance to dietary laws to the prohibition of idolatry. Observance is not legalism — it is covenant faithfulness.
- **Teshuvah (return):** On Yom Kippur, the holiest day of the Jewish year, Jews fast and pray for forgiveness — not for original sin, but for the specific sins of the past year against God and against other people. Sins against other people require direct reconciliation with the person, not just prayer to God.
- **Tzedakah (righteousness/charity):** Often translated "charity" but more precisely "righteousness" — giving is not an act of generosity but of justice. Sharing resources with those in need is a moral obligation, not a supererogatory virtue.
- **No doctrine of hell as primary concern:** Judaism's focus is covenant faithfulness in this world. Gehenna exists in traditional teaching but functions as temporary purification (see Feature 122: Afterlife). The tradition does not spend much theological energy on "how do I avoid hell?"
- **All righteous have a share in Olam Ha-Ba:** The Talmud explicitly states: "All Israel has a share in the world to come" — and, in some formulations, "the righteous of all nations have a share in the world to come." Salvation is not exclusively through Judaism.
- **Justification by faith:** This is essentially not a Jewish category. The closest concept is God's faithfulness to the covenant (*emunah* — trust, faithfulness), which is bilateral: God is faithful to Israel; Israel is called to be faithful to God.

### Hinduism

**The core claim:** *Moksha* — liberation from *samsara* (the cycle of death and rebirth). The obstacle is not sin in the Abrahamic sense, but *avidya* (ignorance of one's true nature) and *karma* (action that perpetuates the cycle). The solution is knowledge, devotion, or right action — depending on the tradition.

**Key doctrines:**
- **The problem:** The fundamental human problem is *avidya* — ignorance of the identity of *atman* (the individual self) and *Brahman* (the ultimate reality). Because of this ignorance, people act from selfish desire, accumulating karma, and continue to be reborn. The cycle is beginningless and driven by craving.
- **Karma:** Not "what goes around comes around" in a vague sense — a precise metaphysical law. Every intentional action creates an impression (*samskara*) that shapes future conditions. Good karma brings favorable rebirths; bad karma brings unfavorable ones. But all karma, good and bad, perpetuates the cycle. The goal is not to accumulate good karma but to transcend karma entirely.
- **Three paths (*margas*):**
  - *Karma yoga* (the path of right action): Acting without attachment to results. Do the right thing because it is right, not because of what you will get from it. The Bhagavad Gita's core teaching (Krishna to Arjuna). Action without ego-investment does not accumulate binding karma.
  - *Bhakti yoga* (the path of devotion): Intense, loving devotion to a personal deity. In the Bhakti tradition (Ramanuja, Chaitanya, Mirabai), the devotee's heart is entirely given to God — Vishnu, Krishna, Shiva, Devi — and God's grace carries the devotee across the ocean of samsara. The closest Hindu parallel to Christian grace.
  - *Jnana yoga* (the path of knowledge/wisdom): Direct inquiry into the nature of reality. Who is the "I" that suffers? What is the nature of the self? The Advaita Vedanta tradition (Shankara, Ramana Maharshi) holds that liberation comes from directly perceiving the identity of atman and Brahman — seeing through the illusion of a separate self.
- **No one right path:** The Bhagavad Gita presents all three paths as valid routes to moksha. Different individuals are suited to different paths. A person may combine them. No single correct method.
- **Grace (anugraha):** In devotional traditions (Vaishnavism especially), God's grace is essential. A person cannot reach God by effort alone; the grace of the guru and the grace of God are necessary.

### Buddhism

**The core claim:** *Nirvana* — the cessation of craving and the end of the rebirth cycle — through the Noble Eightfold Path. There is no permanent self to save, no God to save it, and no grace to receive. The path is the thing.

**Key doctrines:**
- **The problem (the Four Noble Truths):**
  1. *Dukkha*: Life as ordinarily lived is characterized by suffering, dissatisfaction, and impermanence.
  2. *Samudaya*: The origin of suffering is craving (*tanha*) — the desire for things to be other than they are, the desire for permanence in a world of impermanence.
  3. *Nirodha*: The cessation of craving is possible — this is what nirvana is.
  4. *Magga*: The path to the cessation of craving is the Noble Eightfold Path.
- **The Noble Eightfold Path:** Right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration. These eight are not sequential steps but mutually reinforcing practices to be cultivated simultaneously.
- **No self (*anatta*):** The most radical Buddhist claim about the human situation. There is no permanent, unchanging self — the "I" is a process, a flowing stream of experience, not an entity. Suffering is driven by the delusion of a permanent self that needs protecting, feeding, and satisfying. Liberation comes from seeing through this illusion.
- **The role of the teacher:** The Buddha is a teacher and guide, not a savior. He shows the path; the walking is the practitioner's. "Be a lamp unto yourselves; do not rely on external help." However, in Mahayana traditions, Bodhisattvas (enlightened beings who delay final nirvana to help others) play a role that approaches a soteriological function.
- **Theravada vs. Mahayana:**
  - *Theravada*: The path is individual. The goal is personal liberation (*arahat*). The monk is the ideal practitioner.
  - *Mahayana*: The highest ideal is the *Bodhisattva* — one who takes a vow to achieve enlightenment *for the sake of all beings*. Compassion (*karuna*) is inseparable from wisdom (*prajna*). Liberation is not fully achieved alone.
- **Pure Land Buddhism:** Devotion to Amitabha Buddha's compassionate vow enables rebirth in the Pure Land — a realm where conditions for achieving enlightenment are ideal. This is the closest Buddhism comes to grace-based salvation: the practitioner cannot achieve liberation alone but relies on Amitabha's merit. A major school in China, Japan (Jodo Shinshu), and Vietnam.

### Taoism

**The core claim:** *Wu wei* — effortless action in alignment with the Tao. The problem is not sin, ignorance, or karma — it is unnecessary straining against the natural order. The solution is not achievement; it is the removal of what blocks natural flow.

**Key doctrines:**
- **The problem:** The ordinary human problem is artificial, socially conditioned striving — ego, ambition, desire for control, forced cleverness (*wei*). The Confucian and societal emphasis on propriety, performance, and status is, from a Taoist perspective, the disease rather than the cure.
- **Wu wei:** Non-action; effortless action; action that flows from the Tao rather than from ego. Wu wei does not mean passivity — it means acting in harmony with the situation rather than imposing one's will on it. Water is the Tao Te Ching's recurring metaphor: it flows downhill without effort, occupies the lowest places, and over time wears away stone.
- **Simplicity and naturalness (*ziran*):** The goal is to return to the natural state before social conditioning accumulated. "Return to the root." This is why Taoist sages are often described as childlike — not naive, but uncorrupted.
- **No moral framework in the conventional sense:** Classical Taoism is suspicious of explicit moral rules (the Confucian virtues of benevolence, righteousness, propriety). "The great Way is very level; yet people love the byways" (Tao Te Ching 53). When the Tao is lost, virtue is invented; when virtue is lost, kindness is invented; when kindness is lost, rules are invented. Rules are a symptom of lostness.
- **Religious Taoism's salvation:** In religious Taoism (*Tao-jiao*), liberation includes physical immortality, entering the realm of the immortals, or achieving *shen hua* (divine transformation). Ritual, alchemy (internal and external), and the practices of *qi gong* are means of aligning the body's energies with the Tao and ultimately transcending death.

## The Deepest Disagreements

Three questions divide these traditions at the root:

### 1. What is the fundamental problem?
- Abrahamic traditions: moral failure (sin, disobedience, distance from God)
- Indian traditions: cognitive failure (ignorance, delusion — the wrong view of what is real)
- Taoism: social/civilizational failure (unnatural striving against the way things naturally are)

### 2. Does salvation require an external agent?
- Protestant: Yes — God alone saves; the cross is the mechanism.
- Catholic/Orthodox: Yes, primarily — but human cooperation is real.
- Islam: Partially — God's mercy is sovereign, but human effort is required.
- Buddhism (Theravada): No — the path is walked by the individual. (Pure Land Mahayana: Yes — Amitabha's vow.)
- Taoism: The Tao is not an agent. You are not saved *by* the Tao; you return *to* it.

### 3. Is there a permanent self to save?
- All Abrahamic traditions: Yes — the soul is the subject of salvation/damnation.
- Hinduism: Yes (the *atman*) — though Advaita holds that the distinction of "self" and "Brahman" dissolves at liberation.
- Buddhism: No — *anatta* (no-self). The "self" that seeks liberation is part of the delusion. Liberation is the dissolving of the illusion, not the rescuing of the self.
- Taoism: The individual dissolves back into the Tao. The question of whether a "self" persists is not the right question.
