## Problem Statement

The Tracking Faith site traces manuscript traditions, canon debates, and textual transmission — all of which operate at the level of ancient documents. But there is a separate category of evidence: physical objects that have survived in the ground for thousands of years and that corroborate specific biblical figures, events, and claims. These artifacts don't prove miracles — but they prove that the people and places the Bible describes were real, that some events happened roughly as described, and that the biblical authors were working from genuine historical records, not mythology invented centuries later.

A page dedicated to this evidence fills a gap the rest of the site doesn't address.

## The Artifacts

### 1. The Tel Dan Stele — King David

**Found:** 1993–1994, Tel Dan archaeological site, northern Israel.
**Date of artifact:** 9th century BC (c. 841 BC).
**What it is:** A basalt victory monument erected by Hazael, king of Aram-Damascus.
**Key text:** The Aramaic inscription includes the phrase *bytdwd* — almost universally read as "House of David" (*Beit David*).

**Significance:** Before 1993, many scholars classified King David as legendary — a figure inflated by later tradition like King Arthur. The Tel Dan Stele is the first (and still only) extra-biblical inscription that specifically names David's dynasty. It proves that within 150 years of David's death, neighboring kingdoms were tracking the Davidic royal line as a named political entity.

**What it doesn't prove:** It doesn't confirm the specific deeds attributed to David (the slaying of Goliath, the ark of the covenant, the scale of his empire). It confirms he existed and founded a lasting dynasty.

---

### 2. Sennacherib's Prism — The Siege of Jerusalem

**Found:** 1830, Nineveh (modern Iraq).
**Date of artifact:** c. 691 BC.
**What it is:** A hexagonal clay prism (the Taylor Prism) inscribed with the Annals of the Assyrian King Sennacherib, covering his third campaign (701 BC).
**Now in:** British Museum.

**Key text:** *"As for Hezekiah the Jew, who did not bow in submission to my yoke, forty-six of his strong walled towns and innumerable smaller villages in their surroundings I besieged and conquered... Himself I made a prisoner in Jerusalem, his royal residence, like a bird in a cage."*

**The biblical account:** 2 Kings 18–19 and Isaiah 36–37 describe this same siege. The biblical version adds that God struck down 185,000 Assyrian soldiers in the night, causing Sennacherib to withdraw.

**The critical irony:** The Prism confirms Sennacherib's campaign in detail — but *notably does not claim he captured Jerusalem*. He boasts of trapping Hezekiah "like a bird in a cage" — but the cage never closes. The silence around Jerusalem's non-capture aligns precisely with the biblical account that the Assyrian army withdrew without taking the city.

**What it doesn't prove:** The mechanism of the withdrawal (divine intervention vs. plague, military reversal, or political calculation). Both accounts agree that Jerusalem was not captured. They disagree on why.

### 3. Hezekiah's Tunnel — Engineering Confirms the Crisis

**Found:** 1880, Jerusalem (Silwan inscription discovered in the tunnel itself).
**Date of construction:** c. 701 BC.
**What it is:** A 533-meter (1,750-foot) tunnel carved through solid limestone under the City of David, connecting the Gihon Spring (outside the walls) to the Pool of Siloam (inside the walls).

**The biblical account:** 2 Chronicles 32:30 states that King Hezekiah "blocked the upper outlet of the Gihon spring and channeled the water down to the west side of the City of David" in preparation for the Assyrian siege. 2 Kings 20:20 also references the tunnel.

**The Siloam Inscription:** Found carved in the tunnel wall near where the two digging teams met, the inscription describes — in first-person narrative — the moment the two crews heard each other's voices through the rock and broke through. It is one of the oldest examples of prose Hebrew narrative.

**Significance:** Hezekiah's Tunnel is a direct physical confirmation of a specific defensive preparation described in scripture. The tunnel exists, the siege year matches, and the construction method (two teams digging toward each other) is described in both the inscription and the account.

### 4. The Pilate Stone — Pontius Pilate's Title

**Found:** 1961, Caesarea Maritima (the Roman provincial capital of Judea), during excavation of a Roman theater.
**Date of artifact:** c. 26–37 AD.
**What it is:** A limestone dedication block bearing a Latin inscription.

**Key text:** *"...Pontius Pilatus, Praefectus Iudaeae..."* — "Pontius Pilate, Prefect of Judea."

**Significance:** Before 1961, Pilate's existence was attested only in the Gospels, by the Jewish historian Josephus, and by the Roman historian Tacitus. No physical object confirmed him. The Pilate Stone is the first (and still the only) direct epigraphic evidence for Pilate's existence and his specific title.

**What it confirms:** Pilate's name, his title ("Prefect," not "Procurator" — a distinction the Gospel of Luke uses correctly), and his governance of Judea during the period when the Gospels say he was active.

### 5. The Cyrus Cylinder — The Decree That Ended the Exile

**Found:** 1879, Babylon (modern Iraq).
**Date of artifact:** 539 BC.
**What it is:** A barrel-shaped clay cylinder inscribed with the declaration of Cyrus the Great of Persia following his conquest of Babylon.
**Now in:** British Museum.

**Key text:** The cylinder records Cyrus's policy of returning displaced peoples to their homelands and allowing them to restore their temples: *"I returned to these sacred cities on the other side of the Tigris, the sanctuaries of which have been ruins for a long time, the images which used to live therein and established for them permanent sanctuaries."*

**The biblical account:** Ezra 1:1–4 records Cyrus's specific decree allowing the Jewish exiles in Babylon to return to Jerusalem and rebuild the Temple. Isaiah 44:28 and 45:1 identify Cyrus by name — decades before his birth — as God's instrument for restoring Israel.

**Significance:** The Cylinder confirms that Cyrus's "restore the temples" policy was real, systematic, and applied across his empire — not a fabrication invented by Jewish scribes to give their return theological legitimacy.

---

### 6. The Caiaphas Ossuary — The High Priest's Bones

**Found:** 1990, Jerusalem Peace Forest, during construction.
**Date:** c. 1st century AD.
**What it is:** An ornately decorated limestone bone box (ossuary) bearing the inscription "Joseph, son of Caiaphas" in Aramaic.
**Now in:** Israel Antiquities Authority.

**The biblical account:** Caiaphas is named in all four Gospels as the High Priest who presided over the Jewish council that condemned Jesus. He is mentioned by Josephus as high priest from c. 18–36 AD.

**Significance:** The ossuary appears to contain the actual bones of the man who, according to the Gospels, handed Jesus over to Pilate. This is one of the closest physical points of contact between an archaeological artifact and the core Passion narrative.

**Caveat:** The attribution is probable, not certain — "Joseph son of Caiaphas" was not an uncommon name combination. The ornate decoration is consistent with high priestly family status.

### 7. The Dead Sea Scrolls — 1,000 Years of Faithful Transmission

**Found:** 1947–1956, caves near Khirbet Qumran, Judaean Desert.
**Date of artifacts:** c. 250 BC – 68 AD.
**What they are:** Over 900 manuscripts — including the oldest known copies of every book of the Hebrew Bible except Esther. Found in 11 caves by Bedouin shepherds and archaeologists.

**The transmission test:** Scholars compared the **Great Isaiah Scroll** (1QIsa^a, dated to c. 125 BC) with the Masoretic Text copies used by Bible translators — copied approximately 1,000 years later. The texts were nearly identical across a millennium of hand-copying.

**Significance (for text reliability):** The DSS proved that the text of the Hebrew Bible had been transmitted with extraordinary fidelity. The "telephone game" concern — that scribes gradually changed the text — turned out to be largely unfounded for the OT. See the Bible Versions page for how this interacts with the NT textual tradition.

**Significance (for the Apocrypha):** Fragments of Tobit and Sirach were found among the DSS in Hebrew and Aramaic — proving these books were widely regarded as sacred by Jewish sects at the time of Jesus, not later Greek additions.

**The Copper Scroll mystery:** One scroll, found in Cave 3, is made of copper — unlike all the others. It appears to be an inventory of a vast hidden treasure (gold, silver, sacred vessels) buried across dozens of locations in Judea. No treasure matching its description has ever been found. Whether it is literal or symbolic remains debated.

## What Archaeology Cannot Confirm

An honest editorial note matters here:

- **The Exodus** — Large-scale Egyptian records of the period make no mention of an Israelite slave population or a sudden mass departure. Most archaeologists consider the Exodus as described (600,000 men plus families) historically improbable. Some see a historical kernel — a smaller group — beneath a larger narrative.
- **The Flood** — No archaeological evidence supports a global flood c. 4,000 BC. The Black Sea Hypothesis (Ryan & Pitman, 1998) proposes a regional catastrophic flood c. 5,600 BC that may have seeded flood mythology across the Near East.
- **The Virgin Birth and Resurrection** — By definition, these are theological claims, not historical ones. Archaeology cannot confirm or deny them.

The site's editorial standard is the same here as everywhere: name what the evidence shows and what it doesn't, without flattening either claim.

## Open Questions

- Should we include the **Hezekiah Royal Seal** (a clay bulla bearing Hezekiah's name, confirmed in 2015)? It's strong evidence but less narratively interesting than the tunnel.
- Should the **Ketef Hinnom Silver Scrolls** (tiny rolled silver amulets from c. 600 BC bearing the Priestly Blessing from Numbers 6 — the oldest surviving biblical text) get their own card? They confirm the antiquity of the text itself, not just a named person or event.
