## Problem Statement

Most readers assume the Bible is a complete document. It isn't. The authors of scripture cited their sources the way modern writers cite footnotes — by name — and expected their audience to go look them up. At least 20 of those cited sources no longer exist. These aren't suppressed books; they're lost books. The biblical writers assumed they'd always be available. They weren't.

This page addresses a distinct question from every other page on the site: not "what was left out of the Bible?" but "what did the biblical authors themselves say they were referencing — and where is it?"

---

## The Q Source (The Closest to Found)

Before addressing the formally "lost" books, the Q Source deserves special treatment because scholars have reconstructed most of it.

**What it is:** Matthew and Luke share approximately 235 verses of nearly identical content that they could not have both independently invented and that don't appear in Mark (the oldest Gospel). Scholars concluded there must be a common written source — named "Q" from the German *Quelle*, meaning "source."

**What it contained:** Primarily sayings of Jesus — the Beatitudes, the Lord's Prayer, the parables of the Lost Sheep and the Prodigal Son, instructions on prayer and fasting. No narrative of Jesus's birth or resurrection. Just teachings.

**Why it matters:** Q appears to be a *Sayings Gospel* — the earliest Christians were more interested in *how Jesus told them to live* than in the story of his death and resurrection. This predates the full Gospel narrative format. We haven't found a physical copy, but Q is embedded in the Bibles we have today like a ghost — recoverable by comparing Matthew and Luke and subtracting Mark.

## Old Testament — 18 Lost Books Cited by Name

The Hebrew Bible's authors were researchers who cited state archives, royal chronicles, and prophetic collections the way we use footnotes. They assumed these documents would always be available. Most weren't.

### Military & Epic Poetry

| Book | Cited In | What We Know |
|---|---|---|
| **The Book of the Wars of the Lord** | Numbers 21:14 | Epic poetry about Israelite military campaigns during the Exodus. Likely a collection of battle songs. Quoted directly — the author assumes readers can verify the reference. |
| **The Book of Jashar** (Book of the Upright) | Joshua 10:13; 2 Samuel 1:18 | Contains the account of the sun standing still over Gibeon (Joshua) and "The Song of the Bow" — David's lament over Saul and Jonathan. Two of the most dramatic moments in the OT each cite this as their source. |

### Royal Chronicles & State Records

| Book | Cited In | What We Know |
|---|---|---|
| **The Acts of Solomon** | 1 Kings 11:41 | A primary source history of Solomon's reign. The author of Kings explicitly says it contains "all that Solomon did, and his wisdom." |
| **The Annals of the Kings of Israel** | 1 Kings 14:19 (+ 17 other references) | Not the Book of Kings we have — an official state chronicle of the Northern Kingdom. Cited more than any other lost source. |
| **The Annals of the Kings of Judah** | 1 Kings 14:29 | The equivalent southern-kingdom chronicle. Cited repeatedly alongside its northern counterpart. |
| **The Book of the Kings of Israel and Judah** | 2 Chronicles 27:7 | Likely a combined history used as a source by the Chronicles author. May be a different document than the individual annals. |
| **The Manner of the Kingdom** | 1 Samuel 10:25 | A document written by Samuel defining the rules and rights of the new monarchy under Saul. Placed in the ark of the Lord — apparently the ancient equivalent of a constitutional document. |

### Prophetic Collections

| Book | Cited In | What We Know |
|---|---|---|
| **The Book of Samuel the Seer** | 1 Chronicles 29:29 | A prophetic history covering David's reign by Samuel — distinct from the Books of Samuel we have. |
| **The Book of Nathan the Prophet** | 1 Chronicles 29:29; 2 Chronicles 9:29 | Nathan was one of David's closest advisors and the prophet who confronted David over Bathsheba. His own writings: lost. |
| **The Book of Gad the Seer** | 1 Chronicles 29:29 | Gad was David's court prophet. His book apparently covered some of the same ground as Samuel and Nathan's. |
| **The Prophecy of Ahijah the Shilonite** | 2 Chronicles 9:29 | Ahijah prophesied to Jeroboam about the division of the kingdom. His own written prophecy: cited but gone. |
| **The Visions of Iddo the Seer** | 2 Chronicles 9:29; 12:15; 13:22 | Iddo appears to have been a major court historian cited three separate times. All three citations are to lost works. |
| **The Acts of Shemaiah the Prophet** | 2 Chronicles 12:15 | Shemaiah warned Rehoboam not to fight against Jeroboam. His "acts" apparently covered the reign of Rehoboam. |

### Other Lost Documents

| Book | Cited In | What We Know |
|---|---|---|
| **The Annals of Jehu son of Hanani** | 2 Chronicles 20:34 | Incorporated into the Book of the Kings of Israel, according to the citation. Covers the reign of Jehoshaphat. |
| **The Story of the Book of Kings** | 2 Chronicles 24:27 | Referenced as the source for the "many oracles" against Joash. A distinct document from the canonical Kings. |
| **The Acts of Uzziah** | 2 Chronicles 26:22 | Written by the prophet Isaiah. We have the Book of Isaiah — but this separate chronicle of King Uzziah's reign is cited and missing. |
| **The Acts of the Hozai (The Seers)** | 2 Chronicles 33:19 | Reportedly contained King Manasseh's sins, prayers, and visions. The *Prayer of Manasseh* (in the Apocrypha) may be related, but this appears to be a different, longer document. |
| **The Laments for Josiah** | 2 Chronicles 35:25 | A collection of funeral songs for the beloved reforming king Josiah, written by Jeremiah and others. We have Lamentations — but this collection of *specifically* Josiah's laments is cited as a separate document. |

## New Testament — 2 Lost Letters

Paul was a prolific writer. We have 13 (or 7 certainly authentic) of his letters. He references at least 2 others that no longer exist.

| Letter | Cited In | What We Know |
|---|---|---|
| **The "Prior Letter" to the Corinthians** | 1 Corinthians 5:9 | Paul writes: "I wrote to you in my letter not to associate with sexually immoral people." This is a letter sent *before* 1 Corinthians. We've never found it. |
| **The Epistle from Laodicea** | Colossians 4:16 | Paul instructs the Colossians: "When this letter has been read among you, have it also read in the church of the Laodiceans; and see that you also read the letter from Laodicea." Some identify this with Ephesians; most scholars believe it is a distinct, lost letter. |

## What This Tells Us About How the Bible Was Written

The lost books reveal something important about the biblical authors' self-understanding: **they were researchers, not prophets writing in a vacuum.**

When the author of Kings writes "As for the other events of Jeroboam's reign... are they not written in the Annals of the Kings of Israel?" — he is giving a footnote. He expects his 500 BC audience to go to the temple archives and verify the account.

The Bible as we have it is a *surviving remnant* of a much larger literary world. The authors never imagined their library would disappear. The documents they cited with such confidence — royal archives, prophetic collections, epic poetry — were simply lost to warfare, fire, and the passage of time.

---

## Are More Out There?

Archaeologists and scholars believe the answer is yes:
- **Dry desert climates** (Egypt, the Negev, the Judaean Desert) have preserved papyrus for millennia. The Dead Sea Scrolls were found in 1947 because the caves kept them dry.
- **The Herculaneum Scrolls** — a villa buried by Mt. Vesuvius in 79 AD contains hundreds of carbonized scrolls. AI-assisted X-ray imaging (the Vesuvius Challenge, 2023) has begun reading scrolls too charred to unroll. Most are Epicurean philosophy, but a biblical connection is possible.
- **Monastery basements** — many ancient texts were used as binding materials or stored in building walls. New discoveries still emerge from monastery collections (e.g., the recently discovered Syriac manuscripts at St. Catherine's).
