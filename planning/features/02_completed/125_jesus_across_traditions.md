## Problem Statement

Jesus of Nazareth is the only figure who appears as a significant character in multiple distinct world religions — and they disagree fundamentally about who he is. For Christians, he is the second person of the eternal Trinity, crucified and risen. For Muslims, he is one of the greatest prophets, taken alive to God, not crucified. For Jews, he is a 1st-century teacher who did not fulfill the messianic criteria. For Gnostics, he was a divine spirit-messenger who didn't physically suffer. For Hindus and Buddhists, he has been interpreted through their own frameworks — avatar, bodhisattva, great teacher — by individual scholars, though these are not mainstream doctrines.

No other historical figure occupies this kind of contested theological real estate. A page that maps what each tradition actually claims — based on their own primary sources, not strawmen — gives readers genuine clarity about one of the most fraught topics in comparative religion.

## The Traditions

### Protestant Christianity

**The core claim:** Jesus is the Son of God, the second person of the Trinity, fully human and fully divine — *homoousios* (of one substance/being) with the Father. His death was the atoning sacrifice for human sin; his bodily resurrection defeated death. He will return at the Second Coming to judge the living and the dead.

**Key doctrines:**
- **The Incarnation:** The eternal Son of God took on human nature — not by ceasing to be God, but by adding humanity to His divinity. The Council of Chalcedon (451 AD) articulated the classic formula: one person (*hypostasis*), two natures (divine and human), without confusion, without change, without division, without separation.
- **The Hypostatic Union:** Jesus is not half-God and half-human, nor a God who appeared human. He is fully and completely both. This was the target of multiple early heresies: Docetism (Jesus only *seemed* human), Arianism (Jesus was divine but not fully God), Nestorianism (two persons, not one), Eutychianism (the natures merged into one).
- **Substitutionary Atonement:** The dominant model in Protestant theology. Jesus bore the punishment for human sin in the place of sinners. "He was pierced for our transgressions; he was crushed for our iniquities" (Isaiah 53:5). The resurrection is the vindication: death could not hold the sinless one.
- **The Second Coming:** Jesus will return physically, visibly, and bodily (*Parousia*). He will judge all people, resurrect the dead, establish his kingdom, and "be all in all" (1 Corinthians 15:28). Pre-millennialism, post-millennialism, and a-millennialism disagree about the sequence; all agree on the event.
- **The Resurrection body:** Not a spiritual apparition but a physical, transformed body — "the same but different." The disciples recognized him; he ate; Thomas touched his wounds. Yet he passed through locked doors and could vanish. 1 Corinthians 15 is the fullest treatment: a "spiritual body" (*soma pneumatikon*) — not a ghost, but matter transformed.

### Catholic Christianity

**The core claim:** The same core Christology as Protestant theology — fully divine, fully human, crucified and risen. Additional distinctive emphases in Catholic tradition:

**Key distinctions:**
- **Mary as *Theotokos* (God-bearer):** The Council of Ephesus (431 AD) declared Mary the "God-bearer" — not as a statement about Mary per se, but as a statement about Jesus. If Mary is not the mother of God, then the child she bore was not God, which undermines the Incarnation. This has become the basis for the entire Marian theology of the Church.
- **The Real Presence:** In the Eucharist, Christ is truly present — body, blood, soul, and divinity — under the appearances of bread and wine (*transubstantiation*). This is not a symbol or a memorial but the ongoing sacrifice of Calvary, made present again on the altar. "This is my body" (Matthew 26:26) is taken literally.
- **The Church as his body:** The Catholic Church is understood as the mystical body of Christ — the Incarnation continuing in the world. The Pope as successor of Peter (*Petrine succession*) exercises the authority Christ gave to Peter: "You are Peter, and on this rock I will build my church" (Matthew 16:18).
- **Christ as judge:** The Last Judgment (*Dies Irae*) is a dominant Catholic theological and liturgical theme. Every human being will stand before Christ and give an account. The vision of the Last Judgment in Matthew 25 (feeding the hungry, clothing the naked) is central to Catholic social teaching.

### Eastern Orthodox Christianity

**The core claim:** Same Chalcedonian Christology. The distinctive Orthodox emphasis is on the Incarnation itself as the salvific event — not primarily as the mechanism enabling the cross, but as the moment when God permanently united Himself with human nature.

**Key distinctions:**
- **The Incarnation as salvation:** "God became man so that man might become God" (Athanasius). The cross and resurrection are the *completion* of the Incarnation's logic, not its whole meaning. By taking human nature, God has permanently sanctified and elevated it.
- **The Transfiguration:** Christ's transfiguration on Mount Tabor (Matthew 17) is a defining event in Orthodox theology. The disciples saw the uncreated divine light — the same light that is the goal of *hesychast* mystical prayer. The Incarnate Christ reveals divine glory through human matter.
- **The descent into Hades:** Between death and resurrection, Christ descended into Hades and preached to the dead (1 Peter 3:19) — liberating those who had died in faith before his coming. Orthodox iconography depicts this dramatically: Christ harrowing hell, pulling Adam and Eve up from the grave by their wrists.
- **Theosis:** The goal of Christian life is to become like Christ — partaking in the divine nature (2 Peter 1:4). Christ is both the model and the means.

### Islam

**The core claim:** The Prophet Isa ibn Maryam (Jesus, son of Mary) is one of the greatest prophets — the Messiah, born of a virgin, a worker of miracles, given the *Injil* (Gospel) as his revelation — but he is not divine, not the Son of God, and was not crucified.

**Key doctrines:**
- **Isa as prophet:** Jesus is one of the five greatest prophets in Islam (*Ulu al-'Azm*): Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and Muhammad. He is given exceptional honor — the Quran devotes more verses to Mary (*Maryam*) than any other woman, and the whole Surah Maryam (19) covers his birth.
- **The Virgin Birth:** Confirmed in the Quran (3:47, 19:20). Mary conceived without a human father. Jesus's birth without a father is analogous to Adam's creation without parents — both are signs of God's power.
- **Miracles:** The Quran attributes to Jesus: healing the blind and the leper, raising the dead, creating birds from clay and breathing life into them (3:49). These are explicitly described as signs given by God's permission — not evidence of divinity.
- **The denial of the crucifixion:** Quran 4:157 states: "They said, 'We killed the Messiah, Jesus, the son of Mary, the Messenger of Allah.' They did not kill him, nor did they crucify him, though it appeared to them that they did." Most classical Islamic interpretation holds that another person was made to appear like Jesus and was crucified in his place. Jesus himself was raised alive to God.
- **Not divine — shirk:** The claim that Jesus is the Son of God is *shirk* (associating a partner with God) — the one unforgivable sin in Islam. "They have certainly disbelieved who say, 'Allah is the Messiah, the son of Mary'" (Quran 5:72). The Trinity is explicitly rejected: "Say not 'Three'; desist — it is better for you" (Quran 4:171).
- **Return before the Day of Judgment:** Jesus was taken alive to God and is in heaven now. Before the Day of Judgment, he will return to earth, defeat the Antichrist (*al-Dajjal*), break the crosses (symbolically abolishing Christian misconceptions), kill the pig, and establish justice. He will then die a natural death and be buried. This second coming is a shared feature of Islamic and Christian eschatology — with very different implications.

### Judaism

**The core claim:** Jesus was a Jewish teacher, healer, and spiritual figure in 1st-century Galilee. He is not accepted as the Messiah — the messianic criteria were not fulfilled. Views of him range from sincere reformer to false prophet. He is not a significant figure in Jewish theology.

**Key doctrines:**
- **The messianic criteria:** Jewish messianism is specific and concrete — the Messiah will (1) rebuild the Temple in Jerusalem, (2) gather all Jews to the Land of Israel, (3) usher in an era of world peace in which all nations acknowledge God, and (4) bring about the resurrection of the dead. None of these occurred in the 1st century. The Christian response (the "second coming" will fulfill these criteria) is, from a Jewish perspective, a goalpost move — the messianic promise was never conditional on a return visit.
- **The historical Jesus:** Traditional Jewish scholarship has regarded Jesus as a real historical figure — a charismatic teacher, perhaps with some prophetic qualities, who taught within a Jewish context and whose followers drew conclusions that led away from Jewish teaching (divine sonship, supersession of Torah).
- **Jewish-Christian history:** The Jewish relationship to Jesus is inseparable from two thousand years of persecution carried out in his name. The charge of "Christ-killers" (*deicide*), forced conversions, pogroms, the Crusades, and the Holocaust were all carried out by Christian societies. Jewish ambivalence or hostility toward Jesus is inseparable from what has been done to Jews by those who claimed to follow him.
- **Modern Jewish views:** The range is wide — from Orthodox dismissal as irrelevant, to Messianic Judaism (Jews who believe Jesus is the Messiah while maintaining Jewish identity — not accepted as authentically Jewish by mainstream Jewish bodies), to liberal Jewish scholars who have studied Jesus as an important Jewish teacher within his context (Joseph Klausner, David Flusser, Amy-Jill Levine).
- **Maimonides:** The great 12th-century Jewish philosopher and legal authority Maimonides wrote that Jesus and Muhammad, whatever errors they taught, served as instruments for spreading knowledge of the Torah to the nations — not a positive statement, but a grudging acknowledgment of their global historical role.

### Gnosticism (Nag Hammadi texts)

**The core claim:** Jesus was a divine messenger who descended from the True God (*Monad*) to bring *Gnosis* — direct, experiential inner knowledge — to those with "ears to hear." He did not physically suffer on the cross; a divine being cannot be harmed by matter (*Docetism*). His role was not to pay a legal debt but to wake people from the dream of the material world.

**Key doctrines:**
- **Docetism:** From the Greek *dokein* (to seem). Jesus *seemed* to have a physical body but was really a spirit. The physical suffering of the cross was an illusion. In the *Apocalypse of Peter*, Jesus appears laughing on the cross — "the one you see is a living Jesus... the substitute is their shame."
- **The Revealer, not the Redeemer:** In Gnostic Christology, Jesus's primary role is revelation — he reveals the truth of the human condition (divine sparks trapped in material prisons) and the path back to the Pleroma (the divine fullness). The cross is irrelevant to this mission.
- **The Gospel of Thomas:** 114 secret sayings. No Passion narrative. Jesus speaks entirely in wisdom sayings: "The Kingdom is inside of you, and outside of you. When you come to know yourself, then you will be known." The resurrection — in the sense of a bodily return — is not the point.
- **The Gospel of Philip:** Jesus is "the Savior" who brings the knowledge needed for the soul's return to the divine realm. The "Bridal Chamber" sacrament symbolizes the reunification of the soul with its divine counterpart — the goal of the Gnostic path.
- **Mary Magdalene as the primary apostle:** In the Gospel of Mary and multiple Nag Hammadi texts, Mary Magdalene is described as Jesus's closest disciple — the one to whom he revealed the deepest teachings because she was the most spiritually advanced. The conflict between Peter and Mary in the Gospel of Mary captures, with striking candor, the moment the early Church began pushing women out of leadership. (See Feature 118: Nag Hammadi.)
- **The cosmic context:** Jesus in Gnostic Christology is not the second person of the Trinity — he is a being from the Pleroma sent to rescue the divine sparks that fell into matter through Sophia's mistake. His mission is cosmological, not soteriological in the legal sense.

### Hinduism

**The core claim:** Not a mainstream Hindu doctrine, but individual Hindu teachers have interpreted Jesus through Hindu frameworks — most commonly as an *avatar* (divine descent) or as a great *yogi* and spiritual teacher.

**Key interpretations:**
- **Jesus as avatar:** Swami Vivekananda, Paramahansa Yogananda, and other Hindu teachers in the 19th–20th centuries described Jesus as a divine descent — structurally similar to Krishna, Rama, or other *avatars* of Vishnu. An avatar is a deliberate descent of the divine into human form for the purpose of restoring *dharma*. Jesus's life, teachings, and miraculous works fit this framework.
- **Jesus as yogi:** Some Hindu teachers have described Jesus as a great *yogi* who achieved the highest states of spiritual realization — his resurrection, on this reading, was a demonstration of mastery over the physical body achieved through spiritual practice. The Himalayan *tapas* tradition provides a framework: ascetics who achieve mastery over death.
- **Paramahansa Yogananda:** In *Autobiography of a Yogi* (1946), Yogananda extensively interprets Jesus's teachings through the lens of *kriya yoga*, arguing that Jesus and Krishna taught the same essential truths in different cultural forms. This view has been influential in the New Age and Perennial Philosophy movements.
- **Not Hindu doctrine:** These are individual interpretations by Indian teachers engaging with a Western religious figure. They do not represent mainstream Hindu teaching and should not be presented as such. Most Hindus' relationship with Jesus is culturally incidental — he is not part of the Hindu pantheon and has no temple, ritual, or liturgical role.

### Buddhism

**The core claim:** Not a Buddhist figure. Jesus does not appear in any Buddhist canonical text. Some comparative scholars and Western Buddhist-Christian dialogue has noted structural parallels between Jesus and the *bodhisattva* ideal — but these are scholarly observations, not Buddhist teaching.

**Key parallels noted by scholars:**
- **The bodhisattva parallel:** A bodhisattva is an enlightened being who postpones final nirvana to remain in the world and help all beings achieve liberation. The compassion (*karuna*) and self-sacrifice of this ideal has been compared to Jesus's incarnation and atonement. But the framework is entirely different: the bodhisattva is not divine, there is no God to satisfy, and the mechanism (compassionate presence) is not legal substitution.
- **Thich Nhat Hanh:** The Vietnamese Zen teacher and peace activist Thich Nhat Hanh wrote *Living Buddha, Living Christ* (1995), exploring the deep commonalities between Jesus's teachings on love and forgiveness and Buddhist teachings on compassion and inter-being. He emphasizes practice over theology and sees Jesus as someone who was "awake" — a different category from "divine."
- **The Sermon on the Mount:** Buddhist commentators have noted the practical overlap between the Beatitudes and Buddhist ethics — poverty of spirit, meekness, purity of heart, peacemaking. They interpret these as convergent insights about the nature of awakened human life, not as evidence of divine revelation.
- **Tibetan Buddhism:** Some Tibetan teachers have suggested that the soul of Jesus may have achieved a high level of realization in a previous life as a bodhisattva, which would explain his spiritual power. This is a theologically speculative claim within a Tibetan framework, not a mainstream position.

---

### Taoism

**The core claim:** Jesus is not a figure in Taoist teaching, cosmology, or liturgy. He has no role in the Taoist tradition.

**Note for the page:** Several Taoist-Christian comparative scholars have noted parallels between the *Tao* (the impersonal ground of all being) and the *Logos* (the Word/Reason of God in John's Gospel). John 1:1–3 ("In the beginning was the Word") uses *logos* — the same Greek term used by Stoic and Platonic philosophy for the rational principle underlying all existence. Some scholars have suggested that Taoist missionaries or early Christian missionaries in China found the John Prologue and the *Tao Te Ching* mutually illuminating. These are scholarly and interfaith dialogue observations, not Taoist doctrine.

## Why This Article Is Unique

Jesus is the only figure who:
1. Is the central divine figure of one religion (Christianity)
2. Is a major prophet of another religion (Islam)
3. Is historically significant but theologically rejected by a third religion whose own scriptures he claimed to fulfill (Judaism)
4. Was reinterpreted as a divine messenger by an underground movement (Gnosticism) that Christianity actively suppressed
5. Has been voluntarily incorporated into the interpretive frameworks of religions (Hinduism, Buddhism) that had no historical contact with him

No other figure in world history occupies this kind of theological real estate across multiple independent traditions. The disagreements are not primarily about whether he existed (most traditions accept that) but about *what he was* — and those disagreements reflect the deepest structural differences between the traditions themselves.

### Sections (in order)

1. **Introduction** — The unique status of Jesus as a figure claimed and contested across traditions. The editorial goal: map what each tradition actually says, from its own primary sources.

2. **The Traditions** — One section per tradition (8 positions + a note on Taoism). Protestant → Catholic → Eastern Orthodox → Islam → Judaism → Gnosticism → Hinduism → Buddhism → Taoism note.

3. **The Three Core Disputes** — A synthesis section identifying the three deepest points of disagreement:
   - Was he divine? (Christianity: yes / Islam and Judaism: no / Gnosticism: a different kind of "divine" / Hinduism: possibly an avatar)
   - Was he crucified? (All Christian traditions: yes, physically / Islam: no, or someone else was)
   - What was his purpose? (Christianity: atonement / Islam: prophethood / Gnosticism: revelation of gnosis / Hinduism/Buddhism: teaching/awakening)

4. **Why the Disagreements Persist** — A brief editorial note: these are not disagreements that more historical evidence will resolve. They are theological commitments. The question "who was Jesus?" is asked and answered from within each tradition's prior commitments. No tradition will be argued out of its position by additional archaeological data.
