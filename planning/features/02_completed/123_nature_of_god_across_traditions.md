## Problem Statement

"God" is not a single concept shared across traditions with minor variations. The word points at fundamentally different realities depending on who is speaking: a personal being who creates and intervenes (Abrahamic traditions), an impersonal ground of existence that doesn't "create" at all (Taoism), an ultimate reality that contains multiple divine persons (the Trinity), a strict oneness that explicitly rejects any divine persons (Islam), a cosmic consciousness underlying all deities (Hinduism), or a category the tradition deliberately refuses to answer (Buddhism). This page maps those frameworks accurately.

A reader who understands what each tradition actually claims about ultimate reality is better equipped to understand everything else: their ethics, their prayer practices, their afterlife beliefs, and their relationship to other traditions.

## The Traditions

### Protestant Christianity

**The core claim:** One God in three persons — Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — the Trinity. Personal, relational, and self-disclosing through scripture and history. Omnipotent (all-powerful), omniscient (all-knowing), omnipresent (present everywhere).

**Key doctrines:**
- **The Trinity:** Three persons, one substance (*ousia*). Not three gods (tritheism), not one God in three modes (modalism/Sabellianism). The Father, Son, and Spirit are distinct but share fully in the single divine nature.
- **Personal:** God is not an abstraction or a force. He has will, knowledge, love, and purpose. He acts in history (the Exodus, the Incarnation, the Resurrection).
- **Creator:** God created the universe from nothing (*ex nihilo*) — there was no pre-existing matter. Creation is entirely contingent on God's will and sustaining power.
- **Transcendence and Immanence:** God is infinitely beyond creation (transcendent) but also present within it and intimately involved with it (immanent).
- **The Holy Spirit:** The third person of the Trinity. Active in creation, in the inspiration of scripture, and in the transformation of believers (sanctification). In Pentecostal and charismatic traditions, the Spirit's gifts (tongues, healing, prophecy) continue today; in cessationist traditions, the miraculous gifts ended with the apostolic age.

**Key texts:** Genesis 1:1 ("In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth"), Deuteronomy 6:4 (the Shema, which Christianity reframes through the Trinity), Matthew 28:19 (the Trinitarian baptismal formula), John 1:1 ("In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God").

### Catholic Christianity

**The core claim:** The same Trinitarian God as Protestant theology, with additional emphasis on God's presence through the Church, the sacraments, and the role of Mary as *Theotokos* (God-bearer).

**Key distinctions:**
- **Natural theology:** Catholic theology (especially Thomism, following Thomas Aquinas) holds that the existence and some attributes of God can be demonstrated through reason alone, independent of revelation. The Five Ways (cosmological arguments) are the classic expression of this.
- **The filioque:** The Spirit proceeds from the Father *and the Son* (*filioque* in Latin). This clause was added to the Nicene Creed by Western churches at the Council of Toledo (589 AD) — and was the trigger for the Great Schism of 1054. (See Eastern Orthodox section below.)
- **Apophatic and cataphatic theology:** Catholic theology holds both paths: we can say true things about God (cataphatic — "God is good"), but all such statements infinitely fall short of what God actually is. The doctrine of analogy (Aquinas): human concepts apply to God *analogically*, not univocally.
- **God and suffering:** The question of theodicy (why God allows suffering) is a central preoccupation of Catholic philosophical theology, addressed through Augustine, Aquinas, and in the 20th century through figures like Hans Urs von Balthasar.

---

### Eastern Orthodox Christianity

**The core claim:** The same Trinitarian God as Western Christianity — but with a crucial difference on the Spirit's procession, and a deeper emphasis on the unknowability of God's essence.

**Key distinctions:**
- **The filioque controversy:** Eastern Orthodoxy holds that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father *alone*, not from the Father and the Son. This isn't a minor technical dispute — it reflects a fundamental difference in how the Trinity is understood structurally. In the East, the Father is the sole source (*arche*) of the Godhead; in the West, the Father and Son share the role of "spirating" the Spirit.
- **The 1054 Great Schism:** The addition of the filioque to the Nicene Creed without an Ecumenical Council was the formal theological trigger. The political disputes (papal authority, jurisdictional conflicts) were the broader context. The Church has not healed this schism as of today.
- **Essence and Energies:** The Eastern Orthodox distinction between God's essence (*ousia*) and His energies (*energeiai*) is one of the most important contributions of Eastern theology. God's essence is entirely unknowable and beyond participation — no creature can share in it. But God's energies (divine light, grace, love) are real divine acts in which humans can genuinely participate. This is the theological foundation for *theosis* (deification) — humans don't become God's essence, but they genuinely participate in His divine life through His energies.
- **The Tabor Light:** The light the disciples saw at the Transfiguration of Christ is, in Orthodox theology, the uncreated divine energy — the same light that contemplative monks (*hesychasts*) seek to perceive through prayer. Gregory Palamas (1296–1359) defended this teaching against the charge that it created two Gods (one essence, one energy).

### Islam

**The core claim:** *Tawhid* — the absolute, undivided, non-composite oneness of God. Allah has no partners, no associates, no children, no physical form, and cannot be depicted. The Trinity is explicitly rejected as *shirk* (associating partners with God) — the one unforgivable sin.

**Key doctrines:**
- **The 99 Names:** The Quran attributes 99 names/attributes to Allah — *ar-Rahman* (the Most Gracious), *ar-Rahim* (the Most Merciful), *al-Malik* (the King), *al-Quddus* (the Holy), *al-'Alim* (the All-Knowing), and so on. These describe God's attributes in a way that preserves His absolute simplicity — the attributes are real, but they don't divide God into multiple beings.
- **Quran 112 (*Al-Ikhlas*):** The most concise statement of Islamic theology: "Say: He is Allah, the One. Allah, the Eternal, the Absolute. He begot no one, nor was He begotten. And there is nothing comparable to Him." Every clause of this four-verse sura directly addresses a claim Islam rejects: the Trinity (He is One), the claim that God has a Son (He begets not), and the claim that Jesus or any other is divine (nothing is comparable to Him).
- **Transcendence:** God is radically transcendent — *wujud* (existence) belongs properly to God alone; everything else exists contingently. Human language and concepts apply to God only imperfectly. His mercy and love are real, but they are not the same as human mercy and love.
- **No intermediaries:** There is no priesthood and no sacramental system in Islam. Each person has a direct, unmediated relationship with God. Du'a (personal supplication) requires no intermediary.
- **Sufism:** The mystical dimension of Islam (Sufism) introduces concepts like *fana* (annihilation of the self in God) and the "divine beloved" — a language of intimacy that some orthodox scholars have viewed with suspicion. Rumi, Al-Hallaj, and Ibn Arabi represent the spectrum of this tradition.

### Judaism

**The core claim:** YHWH — the God of Israel, strictly and radically one. The Shema is the foundational declaration: *Shema Yisrael, YHWH Eloheinu, YHWH Echad* ("Hear O Israel, the LORD is our God, the LORD is One" — Deuteronomy 6:4).

**Key doctrines:**
- **Strict monotheism:** The Shema is said twice daily by observant Jews. It is the last thing a Jew is meant to say before death. The oneness of God is the central affirmation of Judaism — absolute, with no internal divisions, no son, no partners.
- **The Divine Name:** The four-letter name of God (the Tetragrammaton: יהוה, YHWH) is so sacred it is not spoken aloud. In place of the name, Jews say *Adonai* (Lord) in religious contexts or *HaShem* (The Name) in everyday speech. Many observant Jews write "G-d" rather than the full word in English, to avoid the possibility of the name being erased or treated irreverently.
- **No physical depiction:** Judaism's prohibition on graven images (the Second Commandment) means no physical representations of God. God has no body, no form, no appearance — though the Hebrew Bible uses anthropomorphic language (God's hand, God's face, God's anger). Maimonides argued strenuously that all such language is metaphorical.
- **Relationship and covenant:** God's relationship to Israel is covenantal — a chosen, binding, mutual relationship between God and the Jewish people, established at Sinai. This is not theological abstraction; it is a legal and relational commitment with obligations on both sides.
- **The problem of evil:** The Holocaust (*Shoah*) has generated the most intense Jewish theological engagement with theodicy in history. Responses range from traditional (God's ways are hidden), to new theologies of a limited God, to Elie Wiesel's anguished silence, to the theological move of refusing to give God an answer that excuses the event.
- **Kabbalistic tradition:** In Kabbalah, the *Ein Sof* ("without end") is God in His infinite, unknowable aspect, beyond all description. The *Sefirot* are ten divine attributes/channels through which the Ein Sof relates to and creates the world. Kabbalistic theology is complex and has influenced Jewish mysticism profoundly, though it is not mainstream Orthodox theology.

### Hinduism

**The core claim:** *Brahman* — the ultimate, infinite, impersonal reality underlying all existence. The many personal deities (Vishnu, Shiva, Devi, Ganesh, and hundreds more) are understood as manifestations, aspects, or faces of the one Brahman. Everything is, ultimately, Brahman.

**Key distinctions:**
- **Brahman vs. Atman:** The Upanishads' central teaching: *Atman Brahman* — the individual soul and the ultimate reality are the same thing. "Tat tvam asi" ("That thou art") — the person addressing you is, at the deepest level, the same as the ground of all being. The apparent separateness of selves is *maya* (illusion/appearance).
- **Three main schools:**
  - *Advaita Vedanta* (Shankara): Non-dual. Brahman alone is ultimately real; individual selves and the material world are *maya* — appearances within Brahman. At liberation, the "self" dissolves back into Brahman like a river into the sea.
  - *Vishishtadvaita* (Ramanuja): Qualified non-dual. Brahman is the whole; individual souls and matter are real but are the "body" of Brahman. At liberation, the self remains distinct within God's being, in devotional relationship.
  - *Dvaita* (Madhva): Dualist. Brahman and individual souls are genuinely distinct — always. God is always God; the self is always the self. Liberation is not merger but eternal dwelling in God's presence.
- **Personal deities:** The choice of a personal deity (one's *ishta devata*) is deeply individual in Hinduism. A devotee of Vishnu and a devotee of Shiva may disagree about which is "higher" while both acknowledging the other's deity as real. The tradition accommodates this through the Brahman-as-unity framework.
- **Henotheism or panentheism:** Hinduism is often called *henotheistic* (worshipping one God while acknowledging many) or *panentheistic* (God contains the universe but is not identical to it). Neither Western monotheism nor Western polytheism is quite the right category.

### Buddhism

**The core claim:** Non-theistic. The Buddha explicitly declined to answer questions about whether God exists, describing such questions as unprofitable for liberation. Buddhism's path does not require belief in a creator God and does not depend on one.

**Key distinctions:**
- **The Buddha's silence:** In the *Majjhima Nikaya*, the Buddha uses the parable of a man struck by a poisoned arrow who, instead of accepting treatment, demands to know who shot him, his name, his family, his height, before he will allow the arrow removed. The existential emergency (suffering, the rebirth cycle) is urgent; metaphysical speculation about God is the arrow-identification. It does not help.
- **No creator God:** Buddhism explicitly denies a creator God (*Brahma*) in the sense of a being who created the universe ex nihilo. The universe is beginningless and self-arising. The beings who might call themselves "creators" (*Brahma* gods exist in some Buddhist cosmologies) are simply long-lived heavenly beings who don't remember their own origins.
- **The Three Jewels:** The Buddhist "ultimate" is the *Dhamma* (the truth/the path, not a person), the *Buddha* (as example and teacher, not as God), and the *Sangha* (the community of practitioners).
- **Mahayana Buddha-figures:** In Mahayana Buddhism, *Buddha-nature* (*tathagata-garbha*) is the innate capacity for enlightenment in every being — a teaching that begins to function theologically. Amitabha Buddha in Pure Land tradition is venerated with intense devotion resembling prayer. The *Dharmakaya* (the "truth body" of a fully enlightened Buddha) transcends all individual existence and is in some ways functionally infinite. Some scholars argue that Mahayana Buddhism, especially in its Japanese forms, comes close to theism.
- **Zen:** In Zen, the question of God is simply not the question. Practice is the thing. Conceptual answers — theistic or atheistic — are equally unhelpful.

---

### Taoism

**The core claim:** The *Tao* — the underlying principle, way, or ground of all existence. Impersonal. Unnameable. Self-arising. The Tao did not create the universe; the universe arises from it as water arises from a spring — not designed, not decreed, just so.

**Key distinctions:**
- **The Tao that can be named:** The *Tao Te Ching* opens: "The Tao that can be named is not the eternal Tao." Any description of the Tao immediately falls short of the Tao, because the Tao exceeds all categories. This is a more radical apophatic claim than even the Eastern Orthodox distinction between essence and energies.
- **Not a creator:** The Tao is not a being who decided to make the universe. The universe emerges from the Tao the way shadows emerge from light — not as an act of will, but as an inherent consequence of the Tao's nature.
- **Not personal:** The Tao does not hear prayers in any personal sense. It does not intervene, judge, love, or command. It simply *is* — the way water flows downhill is simply the nature of water.
- **The Virtue of the Tao (*Te*):** Though impersonal, the Tao has a kind of virtue (*Te*) — not a moral law, but a natural excellence. A tree growing tall is expressing its *Te*; a person living in harmony with the Tao is expressing theirs.
- **Religious Taoism:** Popular/religious Taoism developed an extensive pantheon of gods, immortals, and divine bureaucracies — the Jade Emperor, the Eight Immortals, and so on. These are not features of philosophical Taoism (*Tao-jia*) but of religious Taoism (*Tao-jiao*), which developed syncretistically alongside Chinese folk religion and Buddhism.

## The Structural Fault Lines

Three fundamental disagreements cut across all eight traditions:

### 1. Personal vs. Impersonal
The Abrahamic traditions (Protestant, Catholic, Orthodox, Islam, Judaism) all affirm a personal God — a being with will, knowledge, and the capacity to be in relationship. Taoism and philosophical Buddhism describe the ultimate as impersonal — not a being, but a principle or ground. Hinduism contains both: the personal deities are real, but Brahman itself (in Advaita) is ultimately impersonal.

### 2. One vs. Many vs. Non-applicable
Christianity's Trinity (one God, three persons) is distinct from Islam's strict tawhid (one God, zero internal complexity), distinct from Hinduism's one-behind-many, and distinct from Buddhism's non-answer. Judaism and Islam are the strictest monotheisms. Hinduism is the tradition most comfortable with theological plurality. Taoism frames the question differently: "one" and "many" are both categories the Tao exceeds.

### 3. Creator vs. Self-Arising Universe
The Abrahamic traditions all affirm creation ex nihilo — God created the universe freely, from nothing. Buddhism and Taoism both describe a universe with no beginning and no creator. Hinduism (in some schools) describes Brahman as the material and efficient cause of the universe — not creation *from* nothing, but the universe being *of* Brahman's own substance.
